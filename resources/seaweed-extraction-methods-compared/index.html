Alkaline, acid, water or enzyme seaweed extract: what really differs in the bottle

How alkaline, acid, water, enzyme and physical extraction change what ends up in a seaweed extract, and what the research has not settled.

Extraction decides which compounds reach the bottle and in what form. Alkali and acid break polysaccharides into smaller pieces and change polyphenols. Water pulls out what dissolves, with little selectivity. Enzymes, ultrasound and microwave can also shorten polysaccharide chains. No method is settled as best for crops.

By Gafar Laiya and the Gujarat Bio Organics R&D team · Updated 9 November 2026

Why two extracts of one seaweed can differ

The species on a label tells you where a product started. It does not tell you what is in it. A 2019 review of Ascophyllum nodosum says the bioactivity and composition of these products are not all identical and depend on the extraction method. A 2021 review of seaweed extracts says the same for seaweeds in general: method has a great influence on composition.

A 2016 study shows how large the gap can be. Two Ascophyllum extracts were tested on the model plant Arabidopsis. Their polyphenol levels differed four-fold, and they changed gene expression differently. The abstract does not tie this to one process step. Its authors call standardising composition a challenge.

What each method does to the compounds

The table draws on reviews by Shukla and colleagues, Ali and colleagues, Dobrinčić and colleagues, Jönsson and colleagues and Cardoso and colleagues, a 2024 enzyme study and a CSIR-CSMCRI study. Most of this work is on specific species and specific polysaccharides, so treat each row as a direction, not a rule for every seaweed.

Note what the table shows about mild methods. Enzyme treatment is gentle in temperature, yet one study in the Dobrinčić review found it lowered polysaccharide molecular weight in Ecklonia radiata compared with a conventional control. Shorter chains are not the mark of harsh chemistry alone.

What a chemical step adds to the analysis

Alkali and acid do more than extract. In a 2025 tomato pot study of the brown seaweed Cystoseira barbata, the alkali and acid extracts had higher potassium and sulfur than the water extract. The authors attribute this to the potassium hydroxide and sulfuric acid added during preparation.

For a buyer, that matters. A high potassium figure on a certificate of analysis may partly reflect the process chemical, not the seaweed. The same care applies to red seaweeds: Jönsson and colleagues note that alkali treatment of carrageenan removes some sulfate ester groups.

Reviews also describe a trade-off, not simple damage. Ali and colleagues write that polysaccharides are converted into oligomers that are highly bioactive in plants, while hormones may be degraded. Both can happen in one tank.

Does milder mean more active? The studies disagree

The honest answer is that it depends on what was measured.

  • Stronger conditions gave more activity in one test. A study of Macrocystis pyrifera compared alkaline extraction at pH 8 to 12 and 40 to 80°C. Auxin-type activity in a bioassay peaked at pH 11 and 80°C. Tomato seedlings grew fastest with the pH 12, 80°C extract.
  • Heat raised polyphenols in another. Baltic seaweeds boiled for 30 minutes gave more polyphenols than soaking for two days. Mineral levels were similar. Garden cress in Petri dishes was the test plant.
  • Water won in wheat seedlings. In a 2024 study of Cystoseira barbata, water, alkali and acid extracts had different compositions. The water extract at its highest rate gave the most promising seedling results.
  • Alkali and acid did better on tomato growth. In the same group's tomato pots, alkali and acid extracts raised stem length and yield, and water did not change stem length. Only the water extract significantly raised lycopene.

That tomato comparison has limits. The water extract came from dried biomass and the others from fresh. Water went on at 100 mL per pot and the others at 30 mL. There were five pots per treatment. These results describe those setups and are no promise for any product.

What is not settled

  • No best method. A 2025 review states there is no universally effective technique, partly because seaweed biomass varies so much.
  • Yield comparisons are weak. Jönsson and colleagues point out that yield figures ignore differences between species, and that structural integrity matters as much as yield.
  • Hormone claims are hard to pin down. Shukla and colleagues say auxin variation is likely linked to extraction and processing, alongside origin and season. A bioassay shows activity. It does not name the molecule responsible.
  • Conflicting chemistry. Microwave studies report both higher and lower sulfate and molecular weight. Dobrinčić and colleagues link this to protocol, species, origin and harvest time.
  • Enzyme evidence is thin for biostimulants. The 2019 Ascophyllum review found no publications on enzyme extraction of that species. The 2024 enzyme study still calls seaweed research on the method limited.
  • Field evidence is missing. Cardoso and colleagues say most newer technologies have not met large-scale implementation. The tomato study authors say field validation is still needed.

What to ask, and what GBO can say

Since no method wins on paper, ask for process facts and batch data. Ask which species and whether the biomass was fresh or dried. Ask which acid, alkali or enzyme was used, at what pH, temperature and time. Ask whether potassium or sulfur was added in processing. Compare batches on the same tests, and run your own comparison on your target crop before you commit. Our species comparison covers the raw material side.

Frequently asked questions

Which seaweed extraction method is best?

The published evidence does not name one. A 2025 review says there is no universally effective technique. Results depend on the species, the compounds you care about and the test used.

Does alkaline extraction destroy seaweed extract?

It changes it. Reviews report that alkali breaks polysaccharides into smaller oligomers, acts on polyphenols and may degrade small molecules such as hormones. One study on Macrocystis pyrifera still found its highest bioassay activity at high pH.

Is enzyme extraction always gentler?

It avoids harsh chemicals, but it can still shorten polysaccharide chains. Non-specific enzymes can also degrade target compounds. Research on seaweed is described as limited, and seaweed-specific enzymes are hard to obtain.

Why might potassium be higher in an alkaline seaweed extract?

If potassium hydroxide is used, some potassium comes from the process. One tomato study attributed the higher potassium and sulfur of its alkali and acid extracts to the chemicals added.

Is a water extract just weaker?

Not on the available evidence. In one wheat seedling study a water extract gave the most promising results. In a tomato pot study alkali and acid extracts did better on stem length and yield. The setups differed.
